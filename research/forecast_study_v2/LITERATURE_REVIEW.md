Written 2026-10-22 before the v2 protocol was frozen. **A focused, bounded review, not a systematic one.** Verification status per source: **M** = bibliographic record verified in Crossref; **A** = abstract (or the venue's abstract record) read; **P** = article page/body passage read; **—** = not read. ## 1. Scope and how the review was done

Topics: (i) loss functions and the target functional of a point forecast; (ii) intermittent-demand estimators and error measures; (iii) temporal/cross-sectional aggregation in forecasting; (iv) retail-demand benchmarks and boosted trees vs simple/statistical methods; (v) evaluation and inference for forecast comparisons under dependence; (vi) closely related recent work. Sources: Crossref metadata for every DOI, publisher or proceedings pages where reachable, repository/aggregator abstracts for the rest (IDEAS/RePEc, Semantic Scholar, arXiv). ScienceDirect and PLOS pages intermittently blocked automated access (captcha / HTTP 403) and were not bypassed. Full texts were not read except where marked **P**.

## 2. What the literature establishes

**2.1 The optimal point forecast depends on the scoring function.** Gneiting [2011] (**M, A**) shows that comparing point forecasts by an average score can lead to "grossly misguided inferences" unless the scoring function and the forecasting task are matched; squared error is consistent for the mean (Bregman scoring functions) and generalised piecewise-linear scores, of which absolute error is the standard example, are consistent for quantiles; the forecaster should receive the scoring function ex ante or a directive in the form of a functional. Kolassa [2020] (**M, P**) states the practical consequence for forecasting competitions: the "best" point forecast depends on the point-forecast error measure (the expected value minimises squared error; other measures call for other functionals). Koenker & Bassett [1978] (**M**) is the standard reference for quantile regression (absolute-error loss gives the median); the boosting objectives used here follow Friedman [2001] (**M**) and the LightGBM/XGBoost implementations [Ke et al., 2017; Chen & Guestrin, 2016] (**M**).
*Implication for this project:* pooled item-day WAPE is minimised by a conditional median; whether a mean-targeting model "beats" a median-type baseline on that metric is largely determined by theory before any data are seen.

**2.2 Intermittent demand: standard estimators and the unreliability of conventional error measures.** Croston [1972], Syntetos & Boylan [2005] and Teunter et al. [2011] (**M**) define the standard estimators (Croston, SBA, TSB). Hyndman & Koehler [2006] (**M, A**) find that many accuracy measures are "degenerate in commonly occurring situations" and propose the mean absolute scaled error. Prestwich et al. [2014] (**M, A**) argue that almost all known error measures rank forecasters incorrectly on intermittent demand and propose measures that score against the underlying process mean. Kolassa [2016] (**M, A**) argues that point-accuracy measures such as MAD, MASE and wMAPE are poorly suited to low-count retail data and evaluates predictive distributions with proper scoring rules. Kourentzes [2014] (**M, A**) reports that two proposed metrics outperform conventional ones for optimising intermittent-demand methods.
*Implication:* the dependence of rankings on the error measure for sparse demand is documented; WAPE belongs to the criticised family.

**2.3 Aggregation levels in forecasting.** Nikolopoulos et al. [2011] (**M**; abstract via IDEAS — **A** partial) propose aggregating intermittent demand into lower-frequency buckets (fewer zeros) before forecasting and disaggregating afterwards (ADIDA). Athanasopoulos et al. [2017] (**M, A**) introduce temporal hierarchies: forecasts at all temporal aggregation levels are combined into reconciled forecasts that support decisions at different planning horizons. Hyndman et al. [2011] (**M, A**) and Spiliotis et al. [2021] (**M, A**) address cross-sectional hierarchies. The M5 accuracy track asked for lowest-level forecasts that were summed to upper levels [Makridakis et al., 2022a] (**M, A, P**).
*Implication:* that error and ranking depend on the aggregation level, and that aggregation changes the problem for sparse series, is established; the new element a study can add is a *measured, paired* comparison of one learned model and strong baselines across levels.

**2.4 Boosted trees versus simple and statistical methods on retail demand.** In M5, most examined methods and almost all of the top-50 methods with a description were LightGBM-based [Makridakis et al., 2022] (**M, A, P**; corrected 2026-10-22 — an earlier version said "all of the top 50"); Januschowski et al. [2022] (**M, A**) discuss why tree-based methods were so widely adopted and report that they outperformed other approaches in M5; Bojer & Meldgaard [2021] (**M, A**) review six Kaggle competitions with intermittent data and report that gradient-boosted trees and neural networks are strong and that cross-learning ensembles tend to beat local models; Spiliotis et al. [2022] (**M, A** via IDEAS) report lower error and bias for some ML methods on daily SKU demand, with cross-learning helping. Earlier evidence that ML does not automatically beat statistical methods: Makridakis et al. [2018] (**M, A**) found ML methods dominated by eight statistical ones on 1,045 monthly M3 series; M4 [Makridakis et al., 2020] (**M, A**) provides large-scale method comparisons. Reviews: Fildes et al. [2022] (**M**), Petropoulos et al. [2022] (**M**). A recent preprint compares local and global models for intermittent series [Damato et al., 2026] (**arXiv, A**; not peer reviewed when read).
*Implication:* "boosted trees are strong on M5-type data" is established; "they always beat simple methods" is not claimed by the sources read, and Makridakis et al. [2018] show the opposite can hold for other data.

**2.5 Inference for forecast comparisons under dependence.** Hewamalage et al. [2023] (**M, A**) catalogue evaluation pitfalls (partitioning, error calculation, testing). Block resampling for dependent data [Künsch, 1989; Politis & Romano, 1994] (**M**); Holm [1979] (verified through secondary sources); Diebold & Mariano [1995] (**M**). For arrays with crossed dependence (series × time), Owen [2007] (**M, A**) proposes resampling rows and columns separately (the "pigeonhole" bootstrap) and later work describes it as mildly conservative for sparsely sampled unbalanced crossed designs (secondary sources; not verified in the original); Cameron, Gelbach & Miller [2011] (**M**; working-paper abstract read) give a two-way cluster-robust variance estimator. *Implication:* the behaviour of the crossed bootstrap in a dense balanced array of 24 weeks must be checked by simulation, which this study does (`simulation.py`: coverage 0.90–0.98 depending on dependence; mean 0.937; the pigeonhole bootstrap was not conservative in these designs).

## 3. What the literature does **not** establish (the gap, stated conservatively)

1. I did not find, in the sources read, a study that reports on public retail datasets — with held-out, never-scored windows chosen under a written freeze, **endpoint-matched model selection** (selecting on the same metric that is reported), a **paired two-way (series × week) dependence-aware** interval, and an **aggregation ladder on identical forecasts** — the comparison of a boosted model with the best of a large set of simple baselines (naive, seasonal, weekday median/mean, trailing mean/median windows, Croston family, TSB). This is an **absence in what I read**, not proof of absence; a systematic search was not performed.
2. How much of a learned model's advantage or deficit against a strong baseline is *level bias* (a property that aggregate metrics expose) versus *conditional-distribution skill* is implied by the theory but, to my reading, not quantified on these benchmark datasets by the sources above.
3. Whether the result is stable across stores, time windows and horizons under a pre-specified decision rule.

## 4. Gap-and-contribution assessment

| element | status | classification |
|---|---|---|
| Rankings depend on loss/metric/aggregation | established (Gneiting; Kolassa; Prestwich; Hyndman & Koehler; temporal/cross-sectional hierarchy literature) | **not new** |
| Median-type objective suits absolute-error metrics; mean-type suits totals | established theory | **not new** |
| Boosted trees are strong on M5 and Kaggle retail data | established (M5; Januschowski; Bojer & Meldgaard) | **not new** |
| This study: endpoint-matched, validation-selected models vs 16-estimator baseline set, paired dependence-aware inference, never-scored windows, multiple stores/horizons | measures how much and where, under a pre-specified decision rule | **incremental empirical contribution / structured replication-extension of known theory on public data** |
| Bias decomposition of the baseline and the aggregation ladder | descriptive diagnostics | incremental |
| A new method, metric or theorem | none | **no** |

**Conclusion on novelty.** The defensible contribution is an *empirical, reproducibility-oriented evaluation* that quantifies a theoretically expected effect under stricter design and inference than is common, and reports negative and mixed results. It is **not** a new principle. The manuscript will be framed accordingly; the research question is retained but narrowed (see `RESEARCH_QUESTION_AND_CONTRIBUTIONS.md`).

## 5. Limits of this review

Full texts of most cited articles were not read; Holm (1979), Koenker & Bassett (1978) and Friedman (2001) were verified at the metadata level (and Holm via secondary sources); the "mildly conservative" description of the pigeonhole bootstrap rests on secondary sources; no systematic database search, citation chaining or grey-literature search was done; recent (2025–2026) papers were not searched beyond the one preprint above. Reviewers with deeper domain knowledge will likely know additional relevant work.
