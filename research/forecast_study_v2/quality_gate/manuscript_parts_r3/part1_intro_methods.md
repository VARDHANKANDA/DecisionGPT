## 1. Introduction and research question

Boosted trees are strong forecasters of retail demand: in the M5 accuracy competition most of the examined methods, and almost all of the top-50 methods for which a description was available, were LightGBM-based [Makridakis et al., 2022], and tree-based methods outperformed other approaches there [Januschowski et al., 2022]; reviews of Kaggle forecasting competitions, whose data are typically intermittent, report that gradient-boosted trees and neural networks are strong [Bojer & Meldgaard, 2021]. Yet strong aggregate results do not settle whether a learned model beats a *well-chosen simple baseline* on a given error measure, and the answer depends on the measure. Forecasting theory is explicit that the optimal point forecast depends on the scoring function: squared error is minimised by the conditional mean, absolute error by the conditional median [Gneiting, 2011; Kolassa, 2020; Koenker & Bassett, 1978]. For sparse daily demand, whose conditional median is often zero, absolute-error measures therefore reward low forecasts (a consequence of the result just cited, not a finding of this study). Conventional accuracy measures can rank forecasters of intermittent demand incorrectly or be degenerate [Prestwich et al., 2014; Hyndman & Koehler, 2006]; for retail count data Kolassa [2016] argues that MAD, MASE and wMAPE are inherently unsuitable and that proper scoring rules for predictive distributions are needed. The primary endpoint of this study, pooled WAPE, is a wMAPE-type measure and is used because it was prespecified in earlier work of this project; its limitations are discussed in Sections 9 and 10. Aggregating demand over time or items changes the forecasting problem [Nikolopoulos et al., 2011; Athanasopoulos et al., 2017].

A previous study in this project (the "v1 study") found that a LightGBM model with a Tweedie objective did not beat a trailing 28-day median in pooled item-day WAPE on two M5 stores and one retail dataset, while winning after demand was summed over days. An internal audit of that study (carried out by the same AI assistant, so not independent) showed that the contrast was largely the baseline's level bias, that no candidate model targeted a median, and that the evaluation windows had been examined repeatedly. The present work asks the question again under a stricter design and then subjects its own conclusions to a second, adversarial audit (the "quality gate" described in Section 11), whose findings shaped the wording of the claims.

**Research question.** Under model selection fixed on validation data *for the endpoint being reported*, and on evaluation windows that no experiment had scored, does a boosted model beat **the best of 31 prespecified simple baselines**; by how much and with what uncertainty; does the answer change between the item-day and the aggregated endpoint; does the v1 negative result survive when median-type objectives are in the candidate set; does the answer survive adding classical statistical baselines (ETS, ARIMA, Theta) on a fresh window; and does it replicate across stores, horizons and windows? The phrase "best simple baseline" in this paper always means the best of the prespecified baseline set under the stated information set; it does **not** mean the best forecasting method.

## 2. Related work and contribution

The relationship between loss function and optimal forecast [Gneiting, 2011; Kolassa, 2020], the unreliability of conventional error measures for intermittent and low-count demand [Hyndman & Koehler, 2006; Prestwich et al., 2014; Kolassa, 2016; Kourentzes, 2014], temporal and cross-sectional aggregation [Nikolopoulos et al., 2011; Athanasopoulos et al., 2017; Hyndman et al., 2011; Spiliotis et al., 2021], and the strength of boosted trees on M5-type data [Makridakis et al., 2022; Januschowski et al., 2022; Bojer & Meldgaard, 2021; Spiliotis et al., 2022] are established. Evidence that machine-learning methods do not automatically beat statistical ones also exists [Makridakis et al., 2018; Makridakis et al., 2020], and retail forecasting has its own practice and measurement issues [Fildes et al., 2022]. Evaluation guidance is given by Hewamalage et al. [2023]. A preprint compares local and global models for intermittent series [Damato et al., 2026].

The literature work behind this paper is a bounded review (targeted searches and citation following), not a systematic one; its extent and the level at which each source was read are recorded in `quality_gate/REFERENCE_AND_RIGHTS_AUDIT.md`. In the sources reviewed no report was found that combines endpoint-matched validation selection, never-scored windows chosen under a written freeze, a large prespecified baseline set and paired two-way dependence-aware intervals for a boosted model on public retail data; this is a statement about what was read, not evidence that no such work exists. The direction of the main effect follows from theory; **the contribution is incremental and empirical**: the size and location of the effect, its uncertainty, its robustness to dependence, selection and loss asymmetry, and its behaviour on a fresh window against classical competitors. *Not contributions:* a new model, metric or principle; claims about Indian SMEs or other populations; causal effects; customer churn; predictive intervals; decision value.

## 3. Data, preprocessing and data rights

**M5** [Makridakis et al., 2022; Kaggle competition data]. Confirmatory v2 study: daily unit sales of 350 (CA_1, TX_1) and 349 (WI_1) department-stratified random series per store (seed 20261015), drawn among series whose first non-zero sale is on or before day d_1100 (products launched later are excluded). The cut-off is the same for every v2 window and is valid there because the earliest v2 validation block starts after d_1100; it would not be valid for an earlier window, where it would use information from inside the validation/test period (the fresh-window study uses a window-specific cut-off). Items are evaluated on days with a listed selling price; days without a listing carry zero demand by construction (verified: no units were sold on any day without a price in the sampled series), and eligibility is an assumption about what a forecaster knows in advance. **Fresh-window study (v3):** the stores CA_2, TX_2 and WI_2 (350 department-stratified series each, seed 20261022, eligibility cut-off d_848 = first validation day d_933 − 85, the 40 series of a retired benchmark excluded). **Online Retail II** [Chen, 2012; CC BY 4.0]: the 100 SKUs with the largest revenue before the first validation block of each window; daily gross units after documented cleaning (cancellations, non-positive quantity or price, non-product codes and 33,666 exact duplicate lines removed; returns excluded).

**Rights.** The M5 competition rules (read 2026-10-22) permit use of the Competition Data "for non-commercial purposes only, including … for academic research and education" and prohibit transmitting, duplicating, publishing or redistributing it to anyone not participating in the competition. This study uses the data for academic research only. Neither the data nor any array that contains or reproduces M5 sales (stored predictions include the actual values; stored forecasts reproduce forecasts of M5 series) is released. The rules do not address derived forecasts or aggregate statistics: aggregate tables, figures, code and configurations are treated as derived statistics, forecasts alone as not cleared, until the rights holder confirms otherwise. This is a reading of published terms, not legal advice (`quality_gate/REFERENCE_AND_RIGHTS_AUDIT.md`).

## 4. Experimental design and protocol

**Task.** Direct forecasts of daily units for a minimum lag H ∈ {28, 7} days: target day t uses sales history up to t − H plus calendar and (M5) listed-price information of day t; a global model is refit for each 28-day block on rows whose target day lies in [o − 730, o − 1] (o = block start). This is **not** the M5 competition setting (not recursive, not hierarchy-aware, no weighted scaled metric). Predictions are clipped at zero.

**Windows.** A window is defined by the number of trailing days removed before anything is built: W1 (0), W2 (252), W3 (504); in the fresh-window study W4 (756). On the truncated series the last 168 days form six consecutive 28-day test blocks and the preceding 84 days three validation blocks. Roles in the v2 study: **W2 (all four datasets) confirmatory**; W3 (M5) and W1 for the fresh store WI_1 *replications*; W1 for CA_1, TX_1 and Online Retail II a *non-independent re-evaluation* (those periods were scored repeatedly). "Never scored" means that no target value of the evaluated entity-period was scored or used for a decision by any earlier experiment; the periods themselves were used as training rows and, for other series, in a retired benchmark, and a price-identification regression used the targets in-sample (`WINDOW_USE_AUDIT.md`). The design was fixed with knowledge of the v1 results and an internal audit of them. **W4** (M5 stores CA_2, TX_2, WI_2; days 933–1,184, 2013-08-19 to 2014-04-27) is disjoint from W1–W3, was never scored for any entity, and its stores were never evaluated.

**Freezes.** The v2 protocol, code and tests were hashed on 2026-10-22 (08:15:19Z) before any W2/W3/WI_1 validation or test outcome was read (`FREEZE.json`); the v3 protocol and code were hashed before any data of its stores was modelled or scored (`FREEZE3.json`). These are **internal** timestamped hashes, not public pre-registrations. All post-freeze additions, deviations and post-hoc analyses are logged (`DEVIATIONS.md`, `DEVIATIONS_V3.md`).

**Labels.** *Confirmatory*: frozen hypotheses on a window that had not been scored. *Replication*: the same rules on other windows, own Holm adjustment, not pooled. *Non-independent re-evaluation*: periods scored before. *Descriptive*: prespecified summaries without a test. *Exploratory / post-hoc*: analyses added after results were known (the supplementary H3 precision check, the dependence, pinball and selection-robustness analyses of the quality gate). *Separate confirmatory family*: the v3 study.

## 5. Models and baselines

**Learned candidates** (shared base hyper-parameters: 500 trees (XGBoost 400), learning rate 0.05, 31 leaves, subsample 0.8, column sample 0.8; no tuning): LightGBM [Ke et al., 2017; gradient boosting: Friedman, 2001] with Tweedie objective (variance power 1.1, the v1 reference, and 1.5), squared error, absolute error (two variants: 31 leaves; 15 leaves with min. child 100), quantile objective at 0.5; XGBoost [Chen & Guestrin, 2016] with absolute error; ridge regression. Squared error and Tweedie (log link) target conditional means; absolute error and the 0.5 quantile target the conditional median ("median-type"). CatBoost, neural and probabilistic models are not evaluated.

**Selection.** For each endpoint the candidate with the lowest mean validation WAPE of that endpoint over the three validation blocks is selected (ties by name); selection is written to disk before any test block is fitted. Test fits are made only for the two selected models and the reference model (seeds 43 and 44 additionally for the item-day model in W2 and W4). **Validation margins between the selected model and its runner-up are small (Section 8.3); the selected configuration should be read as a representative of its objective class.**

**Simple baselines (31).** Sixteen estimators — naive, seasonal naive, weekday mean and median (4 and 8 weeks), trailing mean (28, 91 days), trailing median (14, 28, 56, 91), Croston [Croston, 1972], Croston-SBA [Syntetos & Boylan, 2005], TSB [Teunter et al., 2011] and an all-zero forecast — each with **matched** information (history up to t − H, exactly the models' information) and **fresh** information (re-estimated at the start of each block or week; 1 to H days old; the all-zero forecast once). Baselines are not tuned beyond the choice among these prespecified variants; the best is selected on validation data for the same endpoint.

**Statistical baselines (fresh-window study only).** Nixtla statsforecast 2.1.1 [no peer-reviewed reference verified]: AutoETS, AutoARIMA (restricted search for compute) and AutoTheta, each with seasonal period 7, fitted on the last 730 days, clipped at zero, with a 91-day-mean fallback for failed fits (counted). Information sets: *matched* (fitted once per block with history through day o − H; never fresher than the learned models, staler later in the block — a handicap) and *fresh* (history through o − 1; at H = 7 staler than the simple fresh baselines, which are refreshed weekly — a handicap to the statistical models in the any-information comparison). They are absent from the v2 study.

## 6. Endpoints and statistical methods

**Endpoints.** Primary: pooled **item-day WAPE** = Σ|ŷ − y| / Σy over eligible (series, day) rows. Aggregate: **subset-day WAPE** = Σ_d |Σ_s (ŷ − y)| / Σ_d Σ_s y (demand of the selected subset summed per day before scoring; not a store total). Descriptive: item-week and subset-week WAPE, relative bias, MAE, RMSE, share of zero forecasts; subset-day WAPE is at least |relative bias|. The estimand is the relative improvement (E_ref − E_model)/E_ref pooled over the six test blocks; the minimum relevant improvement is 2 %. WAPE rewards forecasts near the conditional median on sparse series and is, for count data, criticised by Kolassa [2016]; proper scores and decision costs are not evaluated.

**Hypotheses.** H1 (**the only primary hypothesis**): the item-day-selected model beats the best matched baseline on item-day WAPE. H1b: the same against the best baseline of any information set. H2: the same model beats the v1 reference model (Tweedie 1.1, refit on the same window). H3: the subset-day-selected model beats the best matched baseline on subset-day WAPE. H4 (descriptive): endpoint dependence. H1b, H2 and H3 are secondary; Holm adjustment is applied *within* each family across its cells, not across the four families.

**Inference.** Item-day endpoints: paired crossed bootstrap over series and 7-day blocks (B = 10,000; primary) — the pigeonhole bootstrap of Owen [2007] and Owen & Eckles [2012] — and a two-way cluster-robust delta-method interval with a t(G − 1) reference [Cameron et al., 2011] (secondary). Aggregate endpoint: bootstrap over 7-day blocks of daily totals (iid weeks primary; stationary block bootstrap [Politis & Romano, 1994] secondary; Diebold–Mariano-type tests [Diebold & Mariano, 1995] were not used). The primary methods were fixed by a pre-freeze simulation on synthetic dependence structures (worst-case coverage error 0.050 and 0.057; both mildly liberal; the v1 series-only design collapses). **Decision per cell:** supported iff Holm p < 0.05, the interval excludes 0, improvement ≥ 2 % and the model wins ≥ 4 of 6 blocks; contradicted iff the interval lies below 0; otherwise inconclusive. **Dual-method rule:** a verdict stands only if the secondary method gives the same verdict with its own Holm adjustment. *The two item-day methods use the same cell-total array and both treat weeks as independent clusters, so the rule corroborates against finite-sample and distributional failures, not against weekly dependence; the aggregate pair differs exactly in its treatment of dependence.* Section 8.2 therefore adds a dependence-robust sensitivity analysis. Replication sets are analysed descriptively with their own Holm adjustment and are not pooled with the confirmatory family.
